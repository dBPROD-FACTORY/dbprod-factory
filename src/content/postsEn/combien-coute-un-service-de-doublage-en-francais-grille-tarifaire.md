## The Factors That Drive Dubbing Pricing

A dubbing quote is never a single line item. It adds up several variables, and each one can shift the final bill significantly.

**Program length.** This is the most structural factor. A 30-second spot, a 10-minute e-learning module, a 45-minute series episode, and a 90-minute feature film don't require the same studio resources, artistic direction, or post-production work.

**Number of characters and voices.** Dubbing a documentary with a single narrator has nothing to do with dubbing a series with twelve recurring characters. The more actors there are to cast, direct, and record, the higher the budget climbs, even at an equal program length.

**Adaptation complexity.** A technical text translated word-for-word doesn't demand the same work as an adaptation that has to respect lip-sync, preserve humor, or carry cultural references from the original work. Dialogue adaptation is a craft in its own right, and its complexity weighs directly on the price.

**Turnaround urgency.** A comfortable schedule lets casting, recording, and mixing spread across several weeks. A rush delivery forces several actors and sound engineers to work in parallel — a logical cost, but one you need to anticipate rather than discover on the invoice.

**Usage and distribution rights.** Internal-use dubbing doesn't carry the same rights as national broadcast or worldwide streaming distribution. The broader and longer the exploitation, the higher the fees paid to actors, which raises the project's overall cost.

## The Industry's Standard Pricing Structure

In the dubbing industry, the most common billing logic is a **flat rate per minute of finished program**: the studio quotes a rate per minute of delivered content (translation-adaptation, casting, recording, editing and mixing included), multiplied by the program's total runtime.

This model has the advantage of being transparent and holding the provider accountable for a finished result, rather than billing blind studio hours. It then varies according to the factors above: the per-minute rate for a single-voice spot isn't the same as for an episode with a full cast and fine-grained adaptation.

Volume discounts generally apply on top of this. A project is rarely just one minute of content: a full season, a collection of e-learning modules, or a catalog of spots lets you spread fixed costs (artistic direction, technical setup, project management) over a larger volume, which mechanically lowers the unit rate as the project grows.

## What Budget Should You Actually Plan For?

We could hand you a table of precise figures — but that would be dishonest, given how many variables are in play and how much practices differ from studio to studio. What we can offer is an indicative order of magnitude, built on the logic above rather than a fixed market statistic.

For a **short ad spot** with one or two voices and simple adaptation, you're generally looking at a low range — a few hundred euros. For a **corporate or e-learning module**, the budget climbs with the number of voices and technical demands, while staying reasonable for a business. For a **series episode or documentary**, with multiple characters and real artistic direction, you're more in the range of several thousand euros. And for a **full feature film**, with an extended cast and strict broadcast requirements, the budget can run into the tens of thousands of euros.

These figures give you an intuition, not a quote. Every studio, territory, and project can move meaningfully outside these ranges. The only way to get a reliable number is to send us your specs — exact length, number of characters, deadline, territory, and distribution platform — for a real rate, not a vague estimate.

## How to Control Your Budget Without Sacrificing Quality

A well-managed budget isn't one squeezed to the minimum — it's one that's well informed upfront. Here's how we help clients optimize their costs intelligently.

- **Lock the script before recording.** Every change after casting generates billable retakes; a final script validated upfront avoids costly back-and-forth.
- **Batch your projects.** Several videos, episodes, or modules handled together benefit from volume discounts and shared casting.
- **Plan your deadlines ahead.** A realistic schedule, communicated early, avoids rush surcharges.
- **Be precise about the rights you actually need.** Don't pay for worldwide/all-platform rights if your real usage is limited — but flag it upfront if an extension is likely down the line.
- **Consolidate your feedback.** A single round of consolidated comments always costs less than feedback sent in several waves.

None of these habits cut into artistic or technical quality — they simply eliminate the friction and retakes that inflate a bill without adding value to the final product.
